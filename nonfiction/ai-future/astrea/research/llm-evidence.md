# Evidence notes for AI-futures explainer (checked 2026-09-09)

## Capability measurement and forecasts

- **METR, “Task-Completion Time Horizons of Frontier AI Models”** (last updated 8 May 2026): https://metr.org/time-horizons/ . Defines a model’s *50% time horizon* as the human-expert task duration at which a fitted success curve predicts 50% completion; also reports an 80% horizon. The current page says the benchmark is primarily software engineering, machine learning and cybersecurity, and warns that the metric is task difficulty, not the number of hours an agent can act. Method: estimate human completion times, fit a logistic success-vs-human-time curve for each agent, then read off the 50/80% crossing. Raw data and analysis code are linked on the page. Key uncertainty: narrow/uneven task coverage, setup/scaffolding and human-time estimates; an 8-hour horizon does not mean automating an entire job.

- **METR, “How Does Time Horizon Vary Across Domains?”** (14 July 2025): https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-14-how-does-time-horizon-vary-across-domains/ . Describes the METR-HRS benchmark (HCAST, RE-Bench and SWAA; tasks ranging from 1 second to 16 human-hours), and reports that measured time horizon doubled about every 7 months over 2019–2024, possibly every 4 months in 2024. This is an empirical trend extrapolation, not a forecast of AGI; authors emphasize jagged capability and domain-dependent absolute horizons.

- **METR, “Clarifying limitations of time horizon”** (22 January 2026): https://metr.org/notes/2026-01-22-time-horizon-limitations/ . Useful caveat for UI copy: converting a benchmark horizon into real-world research speedup requires accounting for prompting, waiting, checking outputs and manual work; relative model comparisons still have correlated errors.

- **AI Futures Project, “AI 2027” scenario** (published 3 April 2025; site changelog includes revisions through Feb 2026): https://ai-2027.com/ and research supplements https://ai-2027.com/research . It is explicitly a concrete scenario among many possible futures, informed by trend extrapolation, wargames and expert feedback. Original scenario milestone dates: superhuman coder Mar 2027, superhuman AI researcher Aug 2027, superintelligent AI researcher Nov 2027, ASI Dec 2027 (conditional scenario trajectory). The site later clarifies 2027 was the modal year at publication, while author medians were later (roughly 2028–2032 after a correction); a July 2025 update pushed the median back 1.5 years while retaining 2027 as a serious possibility. Do not present scenario dates as settled predictions. Its “superhuman coder” definition is an AI that can do AI-research coding tasks at the level of the best human company engineer, much faster and cheaply enough to run many copies; ASI is much better than the best human at every cognitive task.

- **Aschenbrenner, “Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead”** (June 2024 PDF): https://situational-awareness.ai/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/situationalawareness.pdf . The essay argues that scaling compute, algorithmic efficiency and post-training (“un-hobbling”) could put AI on course for AI-researcher-level systems by 2027, with feedback from automated AI R&D. This is an author forecast and argument, not a measured result; major uncertainties include data/compute bottlenecks, algorithmic progress, deployment choices, security/geopolitics and whether benchmark ability transfers to open-ended research.

- **“Situational Awareness: A One-Year Retrospective” audit** (public-data snapshot through June 2025): https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/EGGruXRxGQx6RQt8x/situational-awareness-a-one-year-retrospective . The audit reports that global AI investment, electricity use and chip production broadly tracked Aschenbrenner’s forecasts; compute, algorithmic efficiency and un-hobbling looked roughly consistent but were more uncertain; it says xAI’s Grok 3 exceeded expectations by about one-third of an order of magnitude, while some OpenAI/Anthropic models trailed raw-compute trends by ~0.33–0.5 orders and AI revenue growth lagged by months. This is an audit of one-year indicators, not validation of the 2027 endpoint; several indicators are noisy, definitions can shift, and the audit is not an independent controlled evaluation.

- **Grace et al., “Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI”** (arXiv, 2024): https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843 . Survey of 2,778 researchers publishing in top AI venues. Aggregate forecasts gave at least a 50% chance by 2028 for several concrete milestones, but only 10% by 2037 for all occupations to be fully automatable and 50% as late as 2116. Survey probabilities are beliefs, not frequencies; wording and sample selection matter, and “all occupations” differs from AGI/ASI definitions.

## Employment, productivity and macro evidence

- **ILO, “Generative AI and jobs: A 2025 update”** (20 May 2025): https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-2025-update . Refines occupational exposure using task-level data, expert input and AI predictions at the 6-digit occupation level across nearly 30,000 tasks. Reports about one in four workers globally in occupations with some GenAI exposure; most jobs are expected to be transformed rather than made redundant because human input remains needed. Mean automation score fell slightly from 0.30 (2023) to 0.29 (2025), with lower score variance. Exposure is potential task automation, not observed job loss or a forecast of net employment.

- **Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, “Generative AI at Work,” NBER Working Paper 31161** (April 2023; revision Nov 2023; published QJE 2025): https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161 . Quasi-experimental staggered rollout among 5,179 customer-support agents: AI increased issues resolved per hour 14% on average, about 34–35% for novice/low-skilled workers, with minimal gains for experienced/high-skilled workers; authors also report better sentiment/retention. Setting is one firm and one support tool, so it is causal evidence for that workflow, not an economy-wide employment estimate.

- **Noy & Zhang, “Experimental Evidence on the Productivity Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence”** (MIT working paper, 10 March 2023; later Science publication): https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1_0.pdf . Preregistered randomized online experiment with 444 college-educated professionals doing occupation-specific writing tasks. ChatGPT reduced completion time by 0.8 SD and raised quality by 0.4 SD (often summarized as ~40% faster and ~18% higher quality); gains were larger for lower-ability workers. Tasks lacked deep organizational context and fact-checking, limiting extrapolation to jobs.

- **Acemoglu, “The Simple Macroeconomics of AI,” NBER Working Paper 32487** (May 2024): https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487 . Task-based/Hulten-style aggregation: if AI effects mainly come from task-level cost savings, aggregate gains depend on the share of tasks affected and average savings. Using then-available exposure and productivity estimates, he calculates no more than 0.66% TFP gain over 10 years (and about 1.16% GDP in the fixed-labor framework). The result is conditional on assumptions about profitable automation, task exposure and no large new-task or AI-R&D feedback channel; it should be shown alongside higher-upside scenarios, not as a universal ceiling.

- **OECD, “Miracle or Myth? Assessing the macroeconomic productivity gains from Artificial Intelligence”** (OECD AI Papers, 2024): https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/miracle-or-myth-assessing-the-macroeconomic-productivity-gains-from-artificial-intelligence_b524a072-en.html . Builds a micro-to-macro general-equilibrium framework combining task exposure, micro productivity estimates, adoption and input-output linkages. It emphasizes that estimates vary widely and that adoption, new tasks, complementary investment and capability improvements determine aggregate effects; it characterizes Acemoglu’s estimate as cautious. Useful framing: measured task productivity gains do not mechanically imply immediate GDP growth.

## Interpretation guidance

Use separate labels for (1) measured capability, (2) scenario/forecast, (3) exposure potential, (4) causal task-level productivity, and (5) macro projections. Avoid saying “AI can do an X-hour workday” when citing METR, or “X% of jobs will disappear” when citing ILO exposure. Forecast audits can establish whether near-term indicators tracked a prior forecast; they cannot retrospectively prove the long-run scenario endpoint.
