# Astrea physical-economy evidence (checked 2026-09-09)

## IEA: data-centre electricity

- Primary source: IEA, *Energy and AI*, “Energy demand from AI” (report published April 2025; page URL: https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-demand-from-ai). IEA’s base case says global data-centre electricity consumption rises from about 415 TWh in 2024 to about 945 TWh in 2030, “just under 3%” of global electricity consumption. Growth is about 15% per year from 2024–30; accelerated servers, mainly AI-driven, grow about 30% annually and account for almost half of the net increase. IEA says data-centre demand growth is still less than 10% of total global electricity-demand growth in 2024–30.
- Executive summary (same report): https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary . It states 2024 data-centre use was ~415 TWh / ~1.5% of world electricity and that AI is the most important growth driver alongside other digital services. It says the US is the largest source of projected growth, followed by China; in the US, data centres account for nearly half of electricity-demand growth to 2030.
- Definitions/limits: these are IEA modelled global/regional projections, not a metered forecast; base case depends on assumptions about AI uptake, hardware/software efficiency and energy infrastructure. IEA provides sensitivity cases (“Lift-Off”, “High Efficiency”, “Headwinds”). Do not present 945 TWh as a certainty or as AI-only consumption. The separate 2026 update “Key Questions on Energy and AI” projects roughly 950 TWh in 2030 from 485 TWh in 2025: https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary . Use one vintage consistently.

## IFR: industrial robots, China vs world/Japan

- Primary source: IFR, *World Robotics 2025* top-facts page (data released 25 September 2025): https://ifr.org/worldrobotics/report-2025 . It reports 542,000 industrial robots installed globally in 2024; Asia 74%, Europe 16%, Americas 9%. China installed 295,000 (54% of global installations), operational stock exceeded 2 million; Chinese suppliers’ domestic share reached 57%. Japan installed 44,500 and had operational stock ~450,500.
- Exact statistical source: IFR/VDMA, *World Robotics 2025 – Industrial Robots*, executive-summary PDF: https://ifr.org/img/worldrobotics/Executive_Summary_WR_2025_Industrial_Robots.pdf . It gives 542,076 global installations, 4,663,698 operational stock, China 295,045 installations and 2,027,190 stock (43% of global stock), Japan 44,453 installations and 450,530 stock. Method note: consolidated world data reported by robot suppliers plus national robotics associations (including CRIA and JARA). The report is copyright and not a freely reproducible database.
- Definition: IFR’s industrial robot definition is based on ISO: an automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose manipulator programmable in three or more axes (IFR definition page/newsroom: https://ifr.org/home/P44). Counts are installations and operational stock, not robots per worker, capability, utilization, software quality, or embodied value. China’s 54% is share of annual unit deployments, not share of manufacturing output or robot technology.

## Robotics bottlenecks and adoption friction

- IFR, “Top 5 Global Robotics Trends 2025”: https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/top-5-global-robotics-trends-2025 . IFR says SME adoption remains hampered by high initial investment and total cost of ownership; it identifies Robot-as-a-Service as a financing route. This supports a “capital/integration economics” bottleneck, not a claim of a global physical shortage.
- IFR, “Labor Shortage: How to Automate Small and Midsized Enterprises” (31 August 2023): https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/labor-shortage-how-to-automate-small-and-midsized-enterprises . It identifies lack of skilled labour as a strong automation driver and notes that three quarters of EU companies reportedly have difficulty attracting required skills (IFR’s cited EU statistic). Treat this as an adoption/skills constraint and dated evidence, not a 2026 global estimate.
- IFR’s research summary “Automation and the Future of Work”: https://ifr.org/post . It reports establishment-level evidence that profound shortages of skilled workers needed to operate/manage advanced machinery can constrain adoption; it also notes investment risk, integration experience and long procurement/sales processes as barriers in service applications. These are research/industry observations, not a quantified global bottleneck index.

## China rare-earth export controls: what the rule actually says

- Chinese Ministry of Commerce + General Administration of Customs, Announcement No.18 of 2025 (English, issued 4 April 2025): https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/Policies/AnnouncementsOrders/art/2025/art_0dd87cbee7b045bf93fabe6ab2faceee.html . The announcement implements export control on specified medium/heavy rare-earth-related items; exporters must apply for licences under China’s Export Control Law and Dual-Use Item Export Control Regulations. It is a licensing requirement, not text saying that all rare-earth exports are categorically banned.
- Chinese original decision and item schedules: https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zfxxgk/fdzdgknr/ztfl/dwmygl/art/2025/art_99cf6e4b536d4a6796e6e2ed6d79f12c.html . It lists samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium related items, with detailed metals, alloys, oxides, compounds, targets and permanent-magnet materials. Effective/published 4 April 2025; customs commodity references and control codes are in the schedule.
- Practical compliance summary: https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zcfb/blgg/art/2025/art_a6c9fc3558e443d69950a6d814af8c21.html . It states exporters must apply to the competent commerce authority for a licence. “China banned rare-earth exports” would overstate the actual legal mechanism; the defensible statement is controlled items require export licences and approval risk can affect supply timing.
- Limits: the rule controls listed items and transactions under the cited Chinese export-control framework. It does not by itself quantify export volumes, licence approval rates, Japanese exposure, or a permanent cutoff. Later announcements exist; any live 2026 claim must be checked against the current MOFCOM catalogue.

## Japan demography, labour and policy response

- Statistics Bureau of Japan, population estimate at 1 October 2024, published 14 April 2025: https://www.stat.go.jp/data/jinsui/2024np/index.htm . Total population was 123.802 million, down 550,000 year-on-year and declining for the 14th consecutive year. Under-15 share was 11.2% (record low); age 65+ was 36.243 million / 29.3% (record high); age 75+ was 20.777 million / 16.8% (record high). These are estimates, rounded as published; they are not a forecast of 2030 labour supply.
- Statistics Bureau, *Statistical Handbook of Japan 2025*: https://www.stat.go.jp/english/data/handbook/pdf/2025all.pdf . Labour-force chapter reports 15+ population 109.95m in 2024 and labour force 69.57m, up 0.32m; labour-force participation 63.3%. The rise in total labour force despite population ageing is a reminder that demographic pressure does not translate one-for-one into annual labour-force decline.
- METI, *Estimation of the Employment Structure in 2040* (provisional English translation): https://www.meti.go.jp/english/policy/economy/industrial_council/pdf/250603008_03.pdf . The scenario material says current-trend labour supply risks a shortage of about 3m workers responsible for AI/robotics utilization; a “future outlook in 2040 and new direction” scenario assumes AI/robotics utilization and reskilling cover roughly a 2m-worker shortfall. These are scenario estimates, not observed shortages; classifications and assumptions are stated in the PDF.
- METI autonomous delivery-robot policy (26 February 2025): https://www.meti.go.jp/english/press/2025/0226_002.html . METI frames autonomous delivery robots as a response to logistics labour shortages and shopping difficulty; it notes amended Road Traffic Act implementation from April 2023 enabled low-speed small delivery robots on public roads. This is policy/legal context, not evidence of nationwide deployment scale.
- Cabinet Secretariat AI-robotics liaison page: https://www.cas.go.jp/jp/seisaku/ai_robo/index.html and METI robotics policy page: https://www.meti.go.jp/policy/mono_info_service/mono/robot/ . The government’s AI-robotics strategy is explicitly framed around supplementing labour supply in industries facing structural shortages from low birth rates/ageing and raising productivity; the pages show 2026 strategy and implementation-roadmap materials. Treat strategy goals as policy intent, not outcomes.

## Safe synthesis for the website

The evidence supports: (1) electricity and grid connection are a material scaling condition for AI compute; (2) China dominates industrial-robot deployment volume, while Japan has a large installed base and leading manufacturing role; (3) adoption is constrained by skills, integration, capital and procurement economics; (4) China’s 2025 rare-earth measure is a listed-item licensing regime; (5) Japan’s ageing and shrinking population strengthens the case for automation, while policy documents are scenarios/intent. Separate observed statistics, modelled projections, policy intent and conditional inference in copy.
